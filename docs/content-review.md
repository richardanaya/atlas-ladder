# Content review

Every exercise in the seven live section files was read against one standard: it should teach a real Objectivist distinction, have one defensible answer, and explain why the tempting alternatives fail. The catalog was reviewed with them. It names the lessons and does not hold questions, and its objectives were left as they stood.

The old course had 2,101 exercises across 225 lessons. Almost every lesson asked the same claim eight to ten times, as a choice, a match, a blank, and a sentence to tap into order. Feedback named the right answer and stopped. A minority of items were trivia, joke options, or questions about Leonard Peikoff. Quotations were confined to the opening metaphysics and epistemology lessons. Later sections were paraphrase drills with no passage of Rand's to judge.

The replacement is 1,800 exercises, eight in each lesson. A lesson now moves from the claim, to a case, to a rival error, to a harder application. Feedback says why the answer holds and why the plausible miss fails. Verbatim lines are only strings checked against the Ayn Rand Lexicon, and they are listed in `SOURCES.md`. Anything not in that file is marked Paraphrase. Nothing in the live course names Peikoff or *Objectivism: The Philosophy of Ayn Rand*.

## How the counts were made

Reviewed is the old exercise count.

Rewritten means the new question still teaches a point the old lesson already stated.

Removed means the old item was a repeat, a name or citation quiz, a joke or junk option, or a drill the new lesson does not carry. Removed and rewritten add up to reviewed.

Added means the new question brings in a contrast the old lesson never stated: a rival theory, an edge case, or a missing distinction. Rewritten and added add up to the new count.

## Issues by section

### Metaphysics (329 reviewed)

The axioms lesson quizzed the name of the philosophy, the number of branches, and which book a sentence came from. Two items asked what Peikoff calls existence, and another asked for his line on the function of consciousness. Later units restated "the universe exists independent of consciousness" or "a thing is what it is" in ten wordings, with banks such as "weather." Identity was not held to the same respect at the same time, so change could look like a contradiction. Causality was not set against Hume's constant conjunction, chance, or a divine decree, and volition was not kept inside identity. The metaphysically given was a slogan, not Rand's contrast with the man-made. Totality leaned on a "nothing outside" formula that is not her sentence.

What changed: axioms are tested by the fact that a denial has to use them. Primacy of existence is independent of any consciousness, human or divine, and is distinguished from Kant's claim that the mind's forms structure experience. Causality is identity applied to action. The given must be accepted; the man-made must be judged. Focus is the primary choice, and percepts are not chosen. New quotations are from Galt's speech and "The Metaphysical Versus the Man-Made."

### Epistemology (364 reviewed)

The senses unit was the strongest old material, and it still mixed a real point with "which work is this from?" Concepts, definitions, objectivity, certainty, and context were the same sentence repeated. Measurement-omission, genus and differentia, the arbitrary as neither true nor false, and context-dropping were named in the catalog and then not actually tested. Mysticism and skepticism were labels, not arguments. Kant was absent.

What changed: percepts, not stored sensations, are the given. An illusion is a misidentification, not a lying sense. Concepts omit particular measurements and keep the characteristic. Definitions use the fundamental differentia and can be restated as knowledge grows. Objectivity is a chosen adherence to reality by logic. The arbitrary is set aside. Certainty is contextual. Reason is contrasted with mysticism, skepticism, and the noumenal gap.

### Human nature (281 reviewed)

All 281 items were paraphrases. Several lessons drilled "man is a rational animal" or "emotions come from ideas" with junk options (a committee of leaves, the weather, a hobby). None of them said that a need is not a claim on someone else's product, that free will does not rewrite facts, or that an emotion should be neither obeyed nor blanked. One lesson treated "contractual animal" as if it were a quotation on screen. It is Rand's phrase, and it was not in the checked source list, so it is no longer presented as a quotation.

What changed: reason is the means of survival and there is no automatic code. Consciousness is an attribute of a living organism, not a ghost and not an illusion. Emotions register value-judgments. Character is built by repeated choice. Needs are real and are not liens. Other people are dealt with by agreement, not as a tribal organism.

### Ethics (375 reviewed)

The section collapsed the standard of value into one's own life. Egoism was a vocabulary drill on "beneficiary," with the same sentence built and rebuilt, and it never separated production from robbery or kindness from altruism. Sacrifice was "any cost." Honesty was close to a duty to disclose every thought. Happiness was "not a whim," said ten times, without the point that life is the cause and happiness is the result. Feedback did not say why the miss failed. Eleven items used junk banks ("weather," "hobby").

What changed: man's life is the standard, and one's own life is the purpose. The cardinal values are reason, purpose, and self-esteem. The seven virtues stay the seven, with rationality primary. Happiness proceeds from achieved values. Selfishness is concern with one's rational interests. Sacrifice is the surrender of a greater value for a lesser one or a nonvalue. The trader principle is value for value. Quotations are from *The Virtue of Selfishness* and Galt's speech.

### Politics (290 reviewed)

Rights were a slogan that dropped the one fundamental right and confused a contract with a permission. Force items included "Threats are written in special ink" and "Every joke about harm is force." Fraud was fuzzy. Government was "an agent," repeated, and was sometimes flattened into "any government is statism." Capitalism was "the system this unit defends," not the system that follows from rights. Property was a label rather than a right to action. Nineteen items used junk banks.

What changed: a right sanctions freedom of action in a social context. Life is the fundamental right. Property implements it and is not a guarantee of an object. Fraud and a credible threat initiate force because they destroy consent. Insults, low offers, and refusals do not. Government's proper functions are police, courts, and defense, and it may use only retaliatory force. Laissez-faire is the separation of state and economics. Statism is the claim that a person's life belongs to the state.

### Aesthetics (273 reviewed)

The definition of art was copied into ten frames. Distractors included the weather, table manners, and fame. Romanticism was scored as feeling more. A sense of life was scored as a proof. Esthetic judgment collapsed into agreeing with the philosophy. Theme was "not the plot," said again. The need for art was "not a luxury," with no account of why a conceptual consciousness needs it.

What changed: art is a selective re-creation of reality according to metaphysical value-judgments, and a work with no intelligible subject is not art. Its purpose is to show, not to lecture. A sense of life is a pre-conceptual appraisal of man and of existence. Romanticism is the school that recognizes volition, set against naturalism, not against a calm mood. A theme is the meaning the concretes carry. Judgment of the work and agreement with its philosophy are different questions. The definition and the sense-of-life line are quoted from *The Romantic Manifesto*.

### Integration (189 reviewed)

Feedback was one short sentence. Prompts said "this unit" and "build the unit's warning." Banks included joke tokens such as "rhymes." Package deals were defined and then not opened. "Check your premises" was a slogan, not a method. Nothing showed why a principle is not a script, or why a middle idea with its premises cut away cannot be validated or applied.

What changed: the chain runs from metaphysics through epistemology and ethics to politics. A principle is applied to the facts of this case, and the same principle can require a different action when the facts change. Package deals that are actually opened include selfishness glued to brutality, freedom glued to the license to start force, and a right glued to a claim on another person's product. When a conclusion contradicts a known fact, the fact stands and the premise that produced the conclusion is what gets checked.

## Representative before and after

1. Metaphysics, where philosophy starts. Before: "What name did Ayn Rand choose for her philosophy?" with Objectivism, Existentialism, Pragmatism, and Logical positivism. The note said only that she states the name in the preface. After: "What shows that a claim is an axiom, rather than a hypothesis you may set aside?" The right answer is that any attempt to deny it has to accept it and use it. The misses are pragmatism, majority agreement, and revelation.

2. Metaphysics, existence. Before: "Peikoff calls existence which of these?" After: a rock in an unseen canyon. It exists with no awareness required. The misses are idealism, "to be is to be perceived," and a wish.

3. Metaphysics, primacy. Before: "On the primacy of existence, what does the universe depend on?" and a note that awareness is not what keeps it in being. After: the universe exists independent of any consciousness, human or divine, and has a specific identity. A divine author is not a special exception.

4. Epistemology, the senses. Before: the senses, tradition, logic with no observation, or revelation, with a note that pointed at "the second half of that sentence." After: the senses provide the material reason integrates. Emotion used as proof is mysticism. A vote is subjectivism.

5. Ethics, egoism. Before: "On this unit's view, who is the proper beneficiary?" and a note that restated the prompt. After: the actor properly gains, and the interest he acts on has to be one his life as a rational being requires. A stranger's need and a duty to lose are the misses, and they are explained.

6. Ethics, sacrifice. Before: sacrifice as giving up a higher value, with "any feeling of effort" as a miss, and no account of a nonvalue. After: the surrender of a greater value for a lesser one or for a nonvalue, tied to Rand's sentence in "The Ethics of Emergencies." Trading up is not a sacrifice.

7. Politics, fraud and threats. Before: the right idea sat next to "Threats are written in special ink" and "Every joke about harm is force." After: fraud and a credible threat initiate force because they obtain a value by destroying voluntary consent. A rude remark does not.

8. Aesthetics, the definition. Before: the definition, then "any object someone calls useful," and a note that counted the parts. After: the same definition, with the misses named as an unselective record, a fantasy cut off from reality, and a practical object. Each miss drops a different clause. The sentence is quoted from "Art and Cognition."

9. Integration, package deals. Before: "two claims glued," and a note that stopped there. After: the package is distinguished from a real consequence and from a later principle resting on an earlier one, so a student cannot treat every pair of related ideas as a package.
